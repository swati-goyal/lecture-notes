## Key concepts

- **Laser as a source of electromagnetic radiation with special properties** — A laser is a source of electromagnetic radiation whose output has special properties. Such radiation can lie in many parts of the electromagnetic spectrum, not only the visible. Lasers are widely used in electronics and in many applications. [2:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=150s)
- **Wavelength range and examples of lasers** — Laser wavelengths range from fractions of a micrometre to tens of micrometres, so they are not limited to visible light. The CO2 laser was given as an example, emitting at about 10.6 micrometres in the infrared. Another invisible example was mentioned at about 2.7 micrometres, but the transcript is unclear on which laser it is. [3:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=194s)
- **Characteristic properties of laser light** — Laser radiation is described by monochromaticity (very narrow range of colours), directionality (low divergence), and high intensity. Intensity is power per unit area, where power is energy per unit time. [4:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=264s)
- **Monochromaticity and spectral width** — Monochromatic literally means single colour. A real source emits an intensity distribution over wavelength. Its full width at half maximum (FWHM) of that distribution, often written delta lambda, measures how monochromatic it is. A smaller width means a more monochromatic source. [7:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=439s)
- **Broad-spectrum source with filters** — A lamp such as a tungsten halogen lamp emits a broad range of wavelengths. Colour filters or coloured paper let you pick out a band of colour, but the band that passes is still fairly wide, so filtered light is only roughly monochromatic. [9:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=562s)
- **LEDs and the sodium lamp as more monochromatic sources** — A light emitting diode (LED) emits in a particular colour. Its intensity distribution is centred around some wavelength but has a finite width. A sodium lamp is more monochromatic still, with its emission near 589 nm (the transcript suggests the two close lines at about 589.0 and 589.6 nm). These can be measured with a spectrometer or spectrum analyzer. [12:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=741s)
- **Laser light is almost a single frequency** — The lecturer contrasts the sodium lamp, LEDs and coloured light with the laser. Laser light is extremely narrow in wavelength, almost a single frequency, so it is truly monochromatic. The lecturer also noted that quantum mechanics does not permit exactly zero width. The width can become smaller and smaller, but a perfectly single frequency is never reached in a real measurement. [20:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=1224s)
- **Three components of a laser** — A laser has three components: an active medium, a pump (power supply) and an optical resonator made of mirrors that provides feedback. The pumped active medium acts as the amplifier, and the mirrors provide the feedback. [22:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=1369s)
- **Amplifier plus feedback gives an oscillator** — An electronic amplifier uses an active device (such as a transistor) and a power supply, and part of its output is fed back to the input. Amplifier plus feedback gives an oscillator. A laser is the optical analogue: the active medium is the amplifier, the mirrors provide feedback, and optical radiation travelling toward a mirror is partly returned and partly let out. [23:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=1415s)

## Walkthrough

### Welcome and plan for the first lecture [0:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=0s)

- Welcome to the course Introduction to Lasers from IIT Delhi, a basic course on laser fundamentals.
- Today's lecture covers the scope and contents of the course.
- It starts by asking what a laser is and what its various components are.

### What is a laser? Radiation with special properties [2:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=150s)

- A laser is a source of electromagnetic radiation with very special properties.
- The wavelengths span a wide part of the electromagnetic spectrum, from fractions of a micrometre to tens of micrometres.
- Example: the CO2 laser emits at 10.6 micrometres, which is invisible, so lasers are not only visible-light sources.

### Properties: monochromaticity, directionality, intensity [4:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=264s)

- Laser light is characterised by monochromaticity, directionality (small divergence) and high intensity.
- Intensity is power per unit area, and power is energy per unit time.

### Quantifying monochromaticity with spectral width [6:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=377s)

- Monochromatic means a single colour, for example only red or only green.
- For any source the intensity is a function of wavelength. The full width at half maximum of this distribution measures monochromaticity.
- A tungsten halogen lamp has a broad spectrum with intensity spread over many wavelengths.

### Colour filters and LEDs [9:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=562s)

- Coloured filters or paper pass a limited band of colours, but that band is still wide.
- LEDs emit in a particular colour (red, yellow and so on) with an intensity distribution centred on one wavelength.
- A good spectrometer in the laboratory can measure an LED's intensity distribution by scanning wavelength.
- The widths involved are of the order of nanometres.

### Sodium lamp and very narrow widths [15:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=910s)

- A sodium lamp is a more monochromatic source, with lines near 589 nm.
- The two lines are separated by only a fraction of a nanometre. The lecturer said they are seen in an experiment such as interference with a sodium lamp.
- The relative width delta lambda over lambda is extremely small, but quantum mechanics does not permit it to be exactly zero.
- A spectrum analyzer is an important instrument for measuring such spectra.

### Laser vs. other sources [21:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=1298s)

- Sodium lamps, coloured sources and LEDs give essentially one colour with some width.
- A laser line is extremely narrow, almost a single frequency, and so truly monochromatic.
- Lasers are widely used as sources of electromagnetic radiation, including in electronics.

### Components of a laser: amplifier plus feedback [22:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=1369s)

- Three components: active medium, pump, and optical resonator (mirrors).
- An electronic amplifier needs an active device (for example a transistor) and a suitable power supply to amplify the input.
- Feeding part of the output back to the input makes an oscillator.
- In a laser the active medium is pumped, acts as the amplifier, and mirrors provide the feedback. The pump and active medium will be discussed in detail later.

### Course content: Part 1, interaction of radiation with matter [28:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=1730s)

- The course is divided into five parts.
- Part 1 starts with spontaneous emission, stimulated emission and absorption in a medium.
- It then covers line broadening: the mechanisms responsible for the spectral width of a line, homogeneous and inhomogeneous broadening.
- Related topics include lifetime and Doppler broadening, with applications such as Doppler radar.

### Part 2: rates, pumping and population inversion [30:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=1840s)

- Absorption and emission rates, and the condition under which stimulated emission can dominate.
- What a metastable state is and how it helps in laser action.
- Pumping schemes, covering two-, three- and four-level systems.
- Laser rate equations for these systems and the condition for population inversion, which is fundamental to laser action.

### Part 3: optical resonators [31:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=1913s)

- Study of optical resonators, whose characteristics are central to a laser system.
- Starts with simple plane mirror resonators and the stability condition, including its dependence on the separation between mirrors.
- Covers modes, mode transfers and the beam shapes in laser resonators.
- A practical example was mentioned (garbled in the transcript).

### Part 4: amplification, oscillation and laser characteristics [34:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=2074s)

- Laser amplifier and oscillator.
- The threshold condition for oscillation and how the output power depends on pumping power; the maximum output power.
- Elementary characteristics of the laser, including frequency and mode selection, and single-frequency operation.

### Part 5: practical laser systems and applications [35:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=2150s)

- Some practical laser systems are discussed, starting with the ruby laser, and others widely used in industry.
- Different classes of lasers.
- The last topic is using lasers in the laboratory and applications.

### References and prerequisites [37:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WgWozOgMXKo&t=2275s)

- A classic reference is the introductory book by K. Thyagarajan and Ajay Ghatak; the transcript suggests it is on lasers (fundamentals and applications). Specific chapters for the course are marked.
- Other chapters and books serve as reference material, with recent research articles and papers for specific topics.
- Prerequisites: basic knowledge of electromagnetic wave propagation, a little mathematical physics (no very high-level mathematics), and a little electronics.
- An undergraduate-level basic course exposure is sufficient background. The next lecture starts with the first topic.
